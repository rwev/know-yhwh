> "The grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God will stand forever."
> — Isaiah 40:8

The authority of Scripture rests not on human opinion but on its divine origin.
Because Scripture is God-breathed, it carries the authority of God Himself. The Hebrew *davar* (דָּבָר) — "word" — denotes not merely speech but dynamic, effectual power: when God speaks, things happen (Genesis 1:3; Isaiah 55:10–11). Scripture participates in this creative, commanding power of God's own voice.

## The God-Breathed Word

The Greek word *theopneustos* (θεόπνευστος) — "God-breathed" — appears in
2 Timothy 3:16 and is the foundation for the doctrine of Scripture's authority.
The term does not mean that God "breathed into" existing human words but that
Scripture was "breathed out" by God Himself. The text originates from God; it is
His speech committed to writing. Because the Author is the sovereign Lord of all,
His written Word carries absolute authority over every domain it addresses.

### The Self-Attesting Nature of Scripture

Scripture is self-attesting — it does not depend on an external authority to
validate it. Just as God swore by Himself because there was none greater
(Hebrews 6:13), so His Word authenticates itself by its own divine character.

The Westminster Confession puts it well: Scripture's authority "dependeth not upon
the testimony of any man or church, but wholly upon God, the author thereof"
(WCF 1.4). The internal testimony of the Holy Spirit confirms to believers what
Scripture already is in itself (1 John 2:27). We do not believe the Bible because
the church tells us to; we believe the Bible because in it we hear the voice of
the living God.

## Key Doctrines

### Inspiration

Scripture is "breathed out by God" (2 Timothy 3:16). The human authors wrote
freely in their own styles, yet the [Holy Spirit](../../holy-spirit/person-of-the-spirit/) so superintended their writing
that the result is exactly what God intended (2 Peter 1:21). For a fuller treatment, see the article on [inspiration and inerrancy](../inspiration-and-inerrancy/).

### Inerrancy

Because God is truthful and cannot lie (Titus 1:2), His Word is without error
in all that it affirms — in matters of faith, history, and the created order.

### Sufficiency

Scripture contains everything necessary for knowing God, understanding salvation,
and living in obedience. We need not look beyond it for any doctrine essential to
faith and life (2 Timothy 3:16–17).

### Clarity (Perspicuity)

The central message of Scripture — the gospel — is sufficiently clear that any
reader can understand it, though some passages require careful study
(2 Peter 3:16).

## Jesus' View of Scripture

The Lord Jesus treated the Old Testament as the unbreakable Word of God. He
declared that not "an iota, not a dot" would pass from the Law until all is
accomplished (Matthew 5:18). When challenged by His opponents, He asked, "Have
you not read what God spoke to you?" (Matthew 22:31) — treating the written text
as the living voice of God.

In John 10:35, He stated plainly that "Scripture cannot be broken," affirming its
absolute reliability and divine authority. Jesus settled every temptation and
every theological dispute by appealing to "It is written" (Matthew 4:4, 7, 10).
His view of Scripture was total: it was authoritative in every detail, trustworthy
in every claim, and permanent in every promise.

## Scripture and the Church's Tradition

Christians agree that Scripture is the supreme and final authority for faith and
practice — the standard by which every other authority, including the church's
own tradition, must be judged. Traditions differ in how they understand
Scripture's relationship to the church's teaching office and to the accumulated
wisdom of the councils and creeds.

The Reformation articulated this conviction as *sola Scriptura* — "Scripture
alone" — meaning Scripture is the sole *infallible* rule of faith, even as
creeds, confessions, and the church's theological heritage remain valued,
subordinate guides (a position distinct from the more individualistic "solo
Scriptura," which discards those guides and treats each reader as their own
final authority). The Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions hold that
Scripture and Sacred Tradition together form a single deposit of divine
revelation, entrusted to the Church and read through the consensus of the
[Fathers and councils](../../church-history-and-tradition/creeds-and-confessions/)
(2 Thessalonians 2:15; 1 Timothy 3:15). Athanasius, the great champion of Nicene
orthodoxy, could nonetheless appeal to Scripture as the final court: "The holy
and inspired Scriptures are sufficient of themselves for the preaching of the
truth" (*Contra Gentes* 1) — a reminder that reverence for Scripture's supreme
authority runs through the whole Christian tradition, however its relationship
to the church is understood.

Whatever one's position, all major branches of Christianity agree that Scripture
is divinely inspired and carries supreme weight in matters of doctrine and life. For how Scripture is rightly interpreted, see the article on [hermeneutics](../hermeneutics/).
