> "For if their rejection means the reconciliation of the world, what will their acceptance mean but life from the dead?" — Romans 11:15

The Greek word *ekklesia* (ἐκκλησία, "assembly, congregation") — from *ek* ("out of") and *kaleo* ("to call") — denotes a people called out and gathered together. Significantly, the Septuagint (the Greek Old Testament) uses *ekklesia* to translate the Hebrew *qahal* (קָהָל), the "assembly" of Israel before YHWH (e.g., Deuteronomy 9:10; 23:2). This linguistic overlap means that from the earliest Christian usage, the Church was understood not as a brand-new entity but as bound up with Israel's own covenant story — grafted into it, in Paul's image, rather than replacing it. Exactly how the promises God made to [Abraham](../../covenants/abrahamic/), [Moses](../../covenants/mosaic/), and [David](../../covenants/davidic/) continue to relate to Jewish people today, alongside this community of Jews and Gentiles who confess Jesus as Messiah, is a question Christians have thought about in different ways — always mindful that, as Paul says, "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Romans 11:29).

## Common Ground

Whatever else is said, the following convictions are shared across the Christian tradition:

- **God's promises to Israel are irrevocable** — "For the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Romans 11:29)
- **Salvation is through Christ alone** — for Jew and Gentile alike, there is one Savior (Acts 4:12; Romans 1:16)
- **Antisemitism is a grave sin** — the Church's [historical mistreatment](../../world-religions-and-comparative-theology/christianity-and-judaism/) of the Jewish people stands under God's judgment, and has no place in a faithful reading of Scripture
- **God's plan encompasses all nations** — the story of Israel is inseparable from the story of God's love for the whole world
- **The final chapter is not yet written** — God's purposes for Israel and the nations are still unfolding, and believers are called to trust His faithfulness rather than resolve every question

## How Christians Have Understood the Relationship

Reading the same texts, Christians have drawn the lines between Israel and the Church in different places. Some, in a view historically called **supersessionism**, have understood the Church to inherit Israel's covenant promises in full, pointing to Paul's description of believers as "the Israel of God" (Galatians 6:16) and "Abraham's offspring" (Galatians 3:29). A modern minority view, sometimes called **two-covenant theology**, holds that God's covenant with Israel through the Mosaic law remains its own valid path apart from explicit faith in Jesus — though this sits uneasily with the New Testament's consistent presentation of Jesus as the Messiah of Israel first (Romans 1:16), and most Christian traditions do not follow it. **Dispensational** theology, developed in the nineteenth century and influential in much evangelical thought, sees Israel and the Church as distinct peoples with distinct promises, expecting God's promises to ethnic Israel — land, kingdom, temple — to be fulfilled literally in the future. And a view often called **fulfillment** or **expansion** theology, increasingly held across Reformed, evangelical, and Catholic scholarship, sees the Church not as replacing Israel but as being grafted into it through the Messiah (Romans 11:17–24; Ephesians 2:12, 19) — Jesus as the true Israel (Matthew 2:15) through whom Gentiles become "fellow citizens with the saints."

Each of these views is held by Christians who take Scripture seriously and love both the Church and the Jewish people. The tension they are each wrestling with is real: Paul both insists that "not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel" (Romans 9:6) and pleads, in the same letter, that "God has not rejected his people" (Romans 11:2).

## Romans 9--11: The Key Text

Paul's extended argument in Romans 9--11 is the most sustained New Testament reflection on Israel's place in God's plan. Its major movements include:

- **Romans 9:1--5** — Paul's anguish over Israel's unbelief; he lists the privileges of Israel using a remarkable chain of Greek nouns: *huiothesia* (υἱοθεσία, "adoption as sons"), *doxa* (δόξα, "glory"), *diathekai* (διαθῆκαι, "covenants"), *nomothesia* (νομοθεσία, "the giving of the law"), *latreia* (λατρεία, "worship/service"), *epangeliai* (ἐπαγγελίαι, "promises"), the patriarchs, and the Messiah according to the flesh
- **Romans 9:6--29** — God's sovereign freedom in election; "not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel" (9:6)
- **Romans 9:30--10:21** — Israel's stumbling over Christ; salvation by faith for all who call on the Lord
- **Romans 11:1--10** — God has not rejected Israel; a remnant remains by grace
- **Romans 11:11--24** — Israel's stumbling brought salvation to the Gentiles; the olive tree metaphor
- **Romans 11:25--32** — The *mysterion* (μυστήριον): a partial hardening has come upon Israel until the *pleroma ton ethnon* (πλήρωμα τῶν ἐθνῶν, "fullness of the Gentiles" — the full number God intends to save) has come in, and then *pas Israel sothesetai* (πᾶς Ἰσραὴλ σωθήσεται, "all Israel will be saved")
- **Romans 11:33--36** — Doxology: the depth of God's wisdom and knowledge

## "All Israel Will Be Saved" — A Verse Read in Different Ways

Romans 11:26 is among the most discussed verses in all of Pauline scholarship, and Christians have read it in a few different ways. Many take "Israel" in its ethnic sense, consistent with its usage throughout Romans 9--11, and see the verse pointing to a future, widespread turning of the Jewish people to Christ, possibly at or near His return. Others understand "all Israel" as the full number of elect Jewish believers gathered across history, saved progressively as the gospel advances. A less common reading takes "Israel" more spiritually, as including the whole people of God, Jew and Gentile together — though this sits less easily with the consistently ethnic use of "Israel" throughout the surrounding chapters.

Scholars across traditions acknowledge that the passage contains genuine mystery. Paul himself concludes with worship rather than a systematic resolution: "Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and how inscrutable his ways!" (Romans 11:33).
