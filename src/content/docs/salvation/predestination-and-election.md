> "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places, even as he chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and blameless before him." — Ephesians 1:3–4

Predestination and election touch one of the deepest mysteries of the faith: before the world began, God set His love on a people for Himself. The Bible is clear that salvation originates entirely in God's initiative, not in human merit — "he chose us in him before the foundation of the world" (Ephesians 1:4). Exactly how that choosing relates to human freedom and response is something Christians have pondered and described in different ways, and it is a question best approached with humility, since Scripture affirms both God's sovereign choice and the genuine call to "whoever believes" (John 3:16). This doctrine touches the deepest questions of God's [sovereignty](../../gods-nature/sovereignty/), [love](../../gods-nature/love/), and [justice](../../gods-nature/mercy-and-justice/).

## The Biblical Vocabulary

The Hebrew verb *bachar* (בָּחַר) — "to choose, to elect" — is used throughout the Old Testament for God's choice of Israel: "The LORD your God has chosen you to be a people for his treasured possession, out of all the peoples who are on the face of the earth" (Deuteronomy 7:6). This election was not based on Israel's size or worthiness: "It was not because you were more in number than any other people... but it is because the LORD loves you" (Deuteronomy 7:7–8).

In the New Testament, the Greek verb *eklegomai* (ἐκλέγομαι) — "to choose, to select" — and its cognates carry forward this theme. Believers are called *eklektoi* (ἐκλεκτοί) — "chosen ones, elect" (Romans 8:33; Colossians 3:12; 1 Peter 1:1). The verb *proorizō* (προορίζω) — "to predestine, to determine beforehand" — appears in key Pauline texts (Romans 8:29–30; Ephesians 1:5, 11), indicating that God's choice was made before creation and according to His eternal purpose.

## Election in the Old Testament

God's electing activity pervades the Old Testament narrative:

- He chooses Abel's offering over Cain's (Genesis 4)
- He calls Abraham out of Ur and makes him the father of a nation (Genesis 12:1–3)
- He elects Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau (Romans 9:10–13, citing Malachi 1:2–3)
- He chooses Israel from among all the nations (Deuteronomy 7:6; Amos 3:2)
- He selects David, the youngest son, to be king (1 Samuel 16:7–12)

In every case, God's choice is sovereign and gracious — not earned by human merit but flowing from His own mysterious purposes and steadfast love (*chesed*, חֶסֶד).

## Election in the New Testament

The New Testament places election squarely in the context of salvation in Christ:

- Believers were "chosen in him before the foundation of the world" (Ephesians 1:4)
- God "predestined us for adoption as sons through Jesus Christ, according to the purpose of his will" (Ephesians 1:5)
- "Those whom he foreknew he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son" (Romans 8:29)
- Jesus tells His disciples: "You did not choose me, but I chose you" (John 15:16)
- Paul writes: "God chose what is foolish in the world to shame the wise; God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong" (1 Corinthians 1:27)

The golden chain of Romans 8:29–30 traces the sequence of God's redemptive purpose: foreknowledge, predestination, calling, justification, glorification — all presented as accomplished facts from God's eternal perspective.

## What Unites All Christians

Whatever else is said about predestination and election, this is the shared ground on which every Christian tradition stands:

- Salvation is by [grace](../grace/) — no one is saved by their own merit or effort (Ephesians 2:8–9)
- God takes the initiative in salvation — He loved us before we loved Him (1 John 4:19)
- [Faith](../faith/) in Christ is necessary for salvation — "whoever believes in him shall not perish" (John 3:16)
- The [gospel](../gospel/) is to be proclaimed to all people everywhere — "Go into all the world" (Mark 16:15)
- God's character is just and good — He does no wrong (Deuteronomy 32:4)
- The mystery of God's sovereignty and human responsibility is ultimately beyond full human comprehension — "Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and how inscrutable his ways!" (Romans 11:33)

## How Christians Have Reflected on the Mystery

Because Scripture affirms both God's sovereign choice and a genuine human response, faithful Christians have described the relationship between the two in different ways, each seeking to honor what the text says without going beyond it.

The **Reformed** tradition, following Augustine and Calvin, emphasizes God's sovereign initiative: election flows from His good pleasure alone, and the grace that calls the elect is effectual, bringing them surely to faith (Romans 9:11–16; John 6:44). The **Arminian** and Wesleyan tradition emphasizes that God's grace reaches out to all people, enabling a genuine response that He does not coerce — election, in this reading, is God's foreknowledge of those who will freely believe (1 Timothy 2:4; John 3:16). Catholic teaching holds together the reality of predestination and the necessity of human cooperation with grace, a tension explored further within Catholic thought by the Thomist and Molinist schools — the latter, developed by the Jesuit theologian Luis de Molina, proposing that God's "middle knowledge" of what every free creature would do allows Him to ordain the outcome without overriding the will. The **Eastern Orthodox** tradition speaks of *synergeia* (συνέργεια) — divine-human cooperation — resisting any formula that would make God the author of sin or salvation something less than a gift, and holding the mystery of election with reverence rather than resolution.

These differing emphases often meet in Romans 9, where Paul writes of God's purpose in choosing Jacob over Esau "not because of works but because of him who calls" (9:11) and uses the image of the potter and the clay (9:20–21). Some read this as individual election to salvation; others read it as God's sovereign shaping of Israel's role in redemptive history. Both readings are held by serious, faithful interpreters, and Scripture itself moves from this passage into worship rather than resolution: "Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God!" (Romans 11:33).

What matters most for the believer is not settling every question about how election works, but resting in the truth it exists to comfort: that our salvation was secure in God's love before we ever loved Him.
